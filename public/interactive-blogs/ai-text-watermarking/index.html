01 / The problemText has almost nowhere to hide a signal

Images offer many more places to distribute a subtle signal than short text does. A photograph carries millions of pixels, and a change spread thinly across them survives cropping and recompression while staying below the threshold most viewers would notice. A paragraph carries a few hundred tokens, and every one of them is load bearing. Change a word and you may change the meaning.

You could hide characters that do not print. Zero width joiners, unusual spaces, lookalike letters from another alphabet. But normalisation and cleanup tools strip those routinely, often without the user asking, so character based marks are fragile in ordinary use. Anything that survives has to live somewhere a copy and paste cannot reach.

Which leaves an odd question. How do you mark a piece of writing without putting anything into it? The answer is not in the words. It is one level up, in the process that chose them.

02 / Choosing a word

A language model does not write sentences. It writes one token at a time, and a token is often a fragment of a word rather than a whole one.

Given the text so far, the model scores every token in its vocabulary. Those raw scores are called logits. Passed through a softmax they become a probability distribution: how plausible is each token as the next one.

Four candidates shown. A real vocabulary holds tens of thousands.

Now the important part. The model does not simply take the top one. It draws from this distribution, so lychee at 0.30 really does win roughly three times in ten.

Press sample and watch the observed counts converge on the probabilities.

How much freedom there is at a given position has a name: entropy. Spread out probabilities mean high entropy and real choice.

Change the context and the freedom can vanish. After the capital of France is one token takes almost all the mass. That is low entropy, and nothing can be encoded there without breaking the sentence.

So the raw material for a watermark is not the text. It is the leftover freedom at each position, and how much of it exists depends entirely on what is being said.

Everything that follows is about spending that freedom without spending the quality.

03 / The first ideaColour the vocabulary, then prefer one colour

In 2023 a University of Maryland group, with John Kirchenbauer as first author and Tom Goldstein as senior author, published the scheme that started this field. Before each token is chosen, split the whole vocabulary pseudorandomly into a green list and a red list, then push the model towards green.

The trick that makes it recoverable is the seed. Computers cannot produce true randomness on demand, so the split is generated from a seed, and the same seed always gives the same split. Build that seed from something a detector will also have later, namely the preceding tokens, combined with a secret key. Now the colouring can be replayed after the fact by anyone holding the key, and looks like noise to everyone else.

The paper offers two variants, and the difference between them is the whole argument.

03 / Colouring

The seed is a hash of the recent context plus a secret key. Same inputs, same colours, every time. This is the replay mechanism the detector depends on.

That seed splits the vocabulary into green and red. An even split in this example, though the share is a parameter.

A toy vocabulary of 32. The colouring shown is invented, not derived from a real key.

The hard variant simply bans red. Detection becomes trivial, because watermarked text is then entirely green.

Try the switch. Watch what happens to the low entropy case.

And there is the failure. Ban the only correct token and the model must write something false. This is a deliberately restrictive teaching example, not a deployed method.

The soft variant adds a fixed boost to green logits instead of banning red. With a modest boost a strongly preferred token stays overwhelmingly likely, so facts survive.

Drag the boost. Note that a large enough boost will still overturn the leader, so this is a dial, not a guarantee.

Soft marking buys back quality but concedes something: it genuinely reshapes the distribution everywhere, including where the model was already sure. The size of the boost is a judgement call with no principled value.

That is the opening Google set out to close.

04 / Tournament samplingNever touch the odds, only the winner

In October 2024 Google DeepMind published SynthID-Text in Nature. Anthropic says Claude uses a version of this approach. Its move is to stop editing the distribution altogether. Instead of reweighting, it draws several honest candidates from the model's own probabilities and runs a knockout tournament between them, refereeing each match with the secret key.

The example below is the one in Figure 2 of the paper, reproduced faithfully. Watch what the survivors do: they are not all the same token, ties are resolved by coin flip, and each round is refereed by a different function.

04 / The tournament

Draw 2m candidates from the model's real distribution. Here m is 3, so eight seats. They are drawn independently, so tokens repeat in proportion to their probability.

Seats and outcomes reproduce Figure 2 of the SynthID-Text paper.

Round one is refereed by g1, which assigns every token a 1 or a 0 from the seed. In each pair the higher score wins. Equal scores are a genuine tie, broken at random, and four of these matches are ties.

Four survivors, and they are a mix: durian, mango, lychee, mango. Round two uses a different function g2, because the round index is folded into the hash. A token scored 1 a moment ago can score 0 now.

Two survivors. The final is refereed by g3. Mango scores 1, lychee scores 0, so mango is emitted.

The whole bracket runs again for the very next token, with a new seed from the shifted context.

Now the low entropy case, and the reason this design matters. When one token takes essentially all the probability mass, nearly every seat fills with that token and it wins whatever the referee says.

With p = 0.99, all eight seats are that token about 92 percent of the time. Only at p = 1 is it certain.

The paper generally uses m = 30 layers in its experiments. That is a large bracket, though the implementation does not literally build a billion leaves.

More layers add evidence per token, but not without limit: each layer consumes some of the available entropy, so deeper layers carry a weaker mark.

05 / The claim that needs provingWhy the odds survive the tournament

Here is the part that is usually asserted and rarely shown. It is tempting to say every candidate was an honest sample, so the output must be honest too. That does not follow. Under any single key the tournament genuinely does bend the selection probabilities. Green tokens really are more likely to win.

What actually holds is subtler and more interesting: averaged over the key's possible colourings, the original probability comes back exactly. Two tokens, one round, and four equally likely colourings are enough to see it.

05 / Preserving the odds

Take two tokens. A has probability 0.6, B has 0.4. Draw two candidates independently and play one match.

If A is green and B is red, A wins unless both draws came up B. That is 1 − 0.42 = 0.84. A is strongly favoured.

If the colouring flips, A wins only when both draws are A: 0.62 = 0.36. A is now badly hurt. So yes, a particular key really does distort the odds.

When both tokens share a colour there is no preference at all and the tie is broken at random, which returns A its original 0.6. That happens in two of the four colourings.

Average the four equally likely colourings and the distortions cancel exactly: 0.6, the probability we started with.

That is the real claim. Not that any single output is unbiased, but that the scheme is unbiased in expectation over the key.

The paper is careful here, and so should we be. This two competitor configuration is single token non distortionary. Preserving whole sequences additionally needs repeated context masking, and the paper notes that stronger non distortion trades against detectability.

Derivation is exact for this two token, one round case. It does not by itself establish sequence level guarantees.

06 / DetectionWhat a positive result does and does not mean

Detection never sees the bracket. It cannot know which candidates were drawn, which matches were ties, or who won a coin flip. It sees the finished text and it holds the key, and that turns out to be enough, because it can ask a statistical question instead of a forensic one.

Retokenise the passage. For each position, rebuild the seed from the preceding context and the key, replay all m scoring functions, and record what the token that actually appeared scored. Average those values across the passage. The paper's score is exactly this mean g value.

06 / Detection

Every token contributes m replayed verdicts. Under the simplified null model, each has an expected value of 0.5, so an unwatermarked passage averages near a half.

A watermarked passage drifts above it, because its tokens had to keep winning matches where green was favoured. Not to 1: ties, low entropy positions and deeper layers all pull it back.

Dot patterns are authored for illustration. They are not a replay of the bracket above.

Compare the observed mean against the unwatermarked baseline, scaled by the spread. Kirchenbauer's paper uses a z score with a threshold of 4.

Under an ideal null, z above 4 corresponds to roughly one false alarm in 30,000 passages. That is a property of the normal tail, not a measured production rate.

Two things must be counted honestly. Only generated tokens carry evidence, so a prompt pasted in front of the text is not evidence. And the passage must be long enough: short answers, yes or no replies and rigid formats simply do not contain enough decisions.

Which leaves the interpretation, and this is where most coverage goes wrong. The result is provider specific statistical evidence, not a verdict on authorship.

Both error directions are real, and the absence of a mark proves nothing at all.

07 / LimitsWhere the mark thins out or disappears

SituationWhat happens to the mark
Long discursive proseStrongest case. Many high entropy positions, plenty of evidence.
Short replies, yes or no, multiple choiceToo few decisions to carry a signal. Anthropic notes the mark is sparser on factual passages with fewer choices.
CodeWeak. Syntax is near deterministic; the freedom sits mostly in comments and names. Anthropic describes minimal application to code where exact outputs are required.
Light editingSurvives. The score degrades in proportion to how much was changed.
Paraphrase through another modelEffectively erased. The context that seeds every colouring is gone.
Locally run open modelsNo mark. Nothing sits in the sampling loop but the operator.

QuestionsThe things people actually ask

Can I check a piece of text myself?

No. Replaying the colouring requires the secret key. Anthropic's detection API was, as of this page's publication date, in private preview for eligible parties under EU law such as regulators, law enforcement, media and fact checkers, plus enterprises with compliance obligations. Check the source for current status.

Does the watermark identify me?

Anthropic states the mark does not identify a person, an organisation or a conversation. It is evidence about which system produced the text, not about who prompted it.

Does it make Claude's writing worse?

The Nature study compared roughly 20 million watermarked and unwatermarked Gemini responses on user feedback and found differences of 0.01 percent on thumbs up and 0.02 percent on thumbs down, described as statistically insignificant. That is strong evidence from one large deployment, not a universal guarantee for every output.

Is this the same as an AI detector?

No, and the difference matters. A classifier guesses from style and is wrong often. This checks a specific provider's cryptographic mark. It answers a narrow question well rather than a broad question badly.

Why now?

EU AI Act Article 50 transparency obligations took effect on 2 August 2026, requiring providers to mark synthetic content in a machine readable way. Anthropic's rollout note uses that same date as its dividing line for existing models.
