Why you cannot see a watermark in AI text

Anthropic says Claude's writing will carry a hidden signature. There are no invisible characters and no secret metadata. The mark lives somewhere stranger: inside the dice rolls that chose the words.

By Twinkle Garg. Based on Kirchenbauer et al. (2023) and Google DeepMind's SynthID Text (2024). Scroll to explore, or use the diagram’s step buttons.

Watermarking an image is easy. You have millions of pixels, and nudging a few thousand of them by one shade is invisible to any human eye. Text gives you almost nothing to play with. A sentence is a dozen words, and changing one of them can change what it means. "The capital of France is Paris" cannot quietly become "The capital of France is Lyon" just to carry a signature.

You could hide special Unicode characters in the gaps. But text cleanup can strip those, and a watermark that dies to "paste as plain text" is not a watermark. A useful watermark needs some robustness to copying and editing. No method survives every transformation.

So how do you hide something inside ordinary words, without adding anything, without changing the meaning, and still find it later? The answer starts with a detail most people never see: how a language model picks its next word.

Chapter 1 · Choosing a word

Give a language model the start of a sentence and ask it to continue.

The model does not see words. It breaks the text into tokens, small units it can score. Tokens are often fragments of words, but here every token is one full word.

It then produces a probability for every token in its vocabulary that could come next. Tens of thousands of candidates, most of them near zero.

Then it rolls a weighted dice. fox is the likeliest pick, but dog or cat can win too. With sampling enabled, repeated answers can vary.

The winner is added to the text and the whole process runs again for the next token. That small randomness at every step is the crack the watermark slips through. If the model is rolling dice anyway, someone can load them.

Colour the vocabulary

In 2023 a team led by John Kirchenbauer at the University of Maryland published a scheme so simple it sounds like a trick. Before the model picks the next word, split the whole vocabulary at random into two lists. Half go on a green list, half on a red list. Then forbid the red ones.

Chapter 2 · Red list, green list

Here is a slice of the vocabulary as the model sees it: candidates for the next token after The quick brown.

A watermarking function colours every token green or red. Fifty fifty in the original paper, though the share is a dial you can turn.

Red tokens are banned at this step. The model can only sample from the green list. It picks fox.

But if the colouring is random, how can a detector ever know which words were green?

A pseudorandom generator starts from a seed. The same seed reproduces the same sequence. So the seed is built from two things a detector will also know later: the previous token and a secret key.

Now the previous token is fox. New hash, new seed, and the vocabulary is recoloured. The model samples from a fresh green list. Every single token gets its own colouring.

The detector has the finished text, so it knows every previous token. If it also holds the secret key, it can replay the colouring at each position. An unusually green sequence is the signal. In this hard-watermark toy example, every sampled token is green.

There is a catch, and it has a name: entropy. Sometimes the model has real freedom. Sometimes it has none. Put Paris on the red list and the model is forced to write something wrong.

Run this hard version on a small model, ban the words "ring" and "rings", and you get exactly what you would expect.

A softer touch

The same paper offers a fix. Instead of banning red tokens, give the green ones a small boost before the dice roll. A word the model is sure about, like Paris at 99 percent, stays on top even when it is red. But wherever three or four words are neck and neck, the boost quietly tips the choice toward green.

Detection changes with it. You can no longer ask "is everything green?" You ask "is this text greener than chance?" Count the green tokens, subtract what a coin flip would give you, divide by the standard deviation. That number is a z score, and a threshold such as four flags unusually green text. Under an ideal normal null model, the one-sided false-positive probability is about 1 in 31,600. Real thresholds need calibration; repeated contexts and short passages complicate that model.

It works, but it changes the model’s output distribution, and the boost needs tuning. Too small and nothing is detectable. Too large and you are back to banning words. In 2024, Google DeepMind asked a sharper question: what if the watermark preserved the model’s distribution on average, across watermark keys?

Chapter 3 · The tournament

SynthID Text, the method Anthropic says Claude will use, samples first and colours later. Draw eight candidates from the model's real probabilities. Because fox is likeliest, it shows up several times.

Pair them off. Colour the vocabulary. Green beats red. Same colour, coin flip. Half the field is eliminated in round one.

Round two uses a fresh colouring, because the round number is folded into the hash. A token that was green a moment ago may be red now.

One token survives the final. It becomes the next word. A 30-round conceptual bracket has over a billion leaves. The implementation computes the equivalent probability updates efficiently; it does not draw and store a billion candidates for each token.

Why bother? Look at the low entropy case. Ask for the capital of France and all eight seats are Paris. It does not matter who is green. Paris wins, and the watermark cannot damage a word the model was sure about.

Every seat in the bracket was an honest sample. The watermark only decides which honest sample survives.

Chapter 4 · Finding it

The detector never saw the bracket. It only sees the winner. So for every token it replays all 30 colourings and asks one question: in hindsight, how often was this word green?

For a human writer every verdict is a coin flip and the average sits at 0.50. A token that had to survive 30 rounds where green was favoured drifts higher. Not to one. Ties happen, low entropy happens. Across a long enough passage, that drift can become detectable. Short, repetitive, or edited text may not provide enough evidence.

The detector aggregates those scores and compares them with unwatermarked text. SynthID supports several detection methods, including a learned Bayesian detector. These curves illustrate a score shift, not a production detector or calibrated cutoff.

It is not a label that says "Claude wrote this". It is statistical evidence that these words kept landing green far more often than luck allows.

What this means in practice

Google DeepMind reports a large live Gemini experiment with no statistically significant difference in user ratings between watermarked and unwatermarked responses. That supports the approach’s practical quality, while detection still has limits.

  1. The mark lives in freedom. It is only written where the model had several good options. Words it was sure about are untouched.
  2. It can fail. Yes or no answers, multiple choice, lists of facts, very short replies. Not enough freedom, not enough tokens, no signal. Long, varied responses offer more room for a detectable signal.
  3. Removing it is work. Editing and paraphrasing can weaken the signal. How much survives depends on the method, passage length, and type of changes; there is no universal number of words you must replace.
  4. Detection needs access. Checking a provider’s mark requires its key or an authorized detector. Anthropic describes a detection API in private preview for eligible organizations; this article cannot test your text.
  5. Local does not automatically mean unmarked. A local model can use a watermarking sampler too. The presence of a mark depends on the generation setup, not where the model runs.

So the next time someone claims they can spot AI writing by its em dashes, remember where the real signature sits: in the statistical pattern of the word choices, not a visible punctuation habit.
