If every answer had only one possible wording, there would be nowhere to put a watermark.

But language often leaves room. An evening might be warm, pleasant or lovely. A model can use those small choices to leave a repeatable statistical pattern, without inserting invisible characters or attaching metadata.

This is generation-time watermarking: the system participates while the text is written. A general AI-writing classifier instead tries to recognise patterns after the fact, without that generation key.

We’ll start with a green-list scheme, then build up to SynthID Text, the family of methods Anthropic says it uses for Claude’s watermark. These experiments are simplified teaching examples, not a detector for your text. [1]

01 / The raw material

First, there has
to be a choice.

A model generates tokens: words, word fragments or punctuation. Here, we treat each candidate word as one token.

It gives possible next tokens scores, called logits. A function such as softmax turns these into probabilities. A sampler then draws a token using those probabilities.

The most likely token need not win. Draw several times to see the difference between probability and certainty.

Then switch to the factual sentence. Almost all the probability sits on one answer. This is low entropy: little uncertainty, and little freedom for a watermark.

CONTEXT→MODEL SCORES→PROBABILITIES→SAMPLER→NEXT TOKEN

The watermark enters at the selection step.

02 / Make randomness repeatable

Give the choices
a secret pattern.

Imagine assigning candidates to a green or red list. Green does not mean better English. It means favoured by the watermark at this position.

The assignments come from a repeatable function of a key and recent tokens. Change the context and the colours can change. A detector with matching settings can reconstruct them later.

The hard version bans red tokens. That creates a strong signal, but banning the only sensible answer is dangerous. The soft version gives green tokens a boost, then normalises the probabilities again. Red tokens can still win. [2]

Try all three modes, then change the assignment. There is no permanent list of “AI words”. A modest boost can preserve a strong favourite; a very large boost can overwhelm it.

“Plausible next word” and “fits the secret pattern” are two different scores.

The distinction that makes the rest work

03 / A more careful sampler

Let the candidates
play a tournament.

SynthID offers another way to use that freedom. Picture eight candidates sampled from the original probabilities, with repeats allowed.

Pair them up. Green beats red. If colours match, break the tie randomly. Recolour survivors with a new key-derived scoring function for the next round. Repeat until one token remains. [3]

Follow the lines. Copies of the same token have the same colour within a round, but that colour can change between rounds.

Switch to Only Paris. If Paris has probability exactly 1, every candidate is Paris. The tournament cannot select anything else. At 99%, alternatives remain possible.

Does a real system build a billion leaves?

No. Our example has three rounds and eight leaves. The paper generally uses 30 rounds in its experiments, but the implementation computes equivalent probability updates without materialising 2³⁰ candidates. Thirty is not a confirmed Claude production setting.

The surprising part

A preference per key.
A balance across keys.

Sampling candidates from the model does not, by itself, prove the final probabilities are unchanged. The balance comes from the symmetry of random scores.

Take one two-candidate match. A starts at 60%; B at 40%. A’s chance of winning depends on their colours:

A favoured84%A wins if either draw is A
B favoured36%A wins only if both draws are A
Both green60%Random tie-breaking
Both red60%The same fair tie rule

Four equally likely assignments, averaged:
(84 + 36 + 60 + 60) ÷ 4 = 60%

This demonstrates one round with independent fair assignments. Sequence-level guarantees need further handling, including repeated-context masking. SynthID also offers configurations that trade some quality for stronger detection; preservation is not universal. [3]

04 / Read the trace

A clue in one word.
Evidence in many.

The detector sees the text, not the tournament. It retokenises the passage and uses the matching key, recent context and algorithm settings to reconstruct scores for tokens that actually appeared.

Under an unwatermarked baseline, a fair binary score has expected value 0.5. A watermarked passage tends to score higher. Longer, varied passages provide more opportunities to see that difference.

This experiment isolates the statistical idea. Every square is one independent simulated observation. Increase the length, or replace observations with unwatermarked ones.

Real SynthID detection combines scores across rounds and eligible tokens, handles repetition, and supports methods such as learned Bayesian scoring. This coin model is not its production detector. [3]

05 / Evidence has boundaries

A statistical signal.
Not a verdict on a person.

+

Signal detected

The passage is unusually consistent with the tested mark. That can support a provider’s involvement, including substantial editing. It does not establish who wrote every word.

?

No strong signal

The text may be human-written, from another model, too short, repetitive or heavily edited. No detected watermark does not prove human authorship.

≠

Probability is not authorship

A 1% chance under the unwatermarked model is not a 99% probability that AI wrote the text. Interpretation needs calibrated error rates and context.

A false positive flags unwatermarked text. A false negative misses a genuine mark. Neither disappears because a diagram looks convincing.

06 / Back in the real world

What survives.
What doesn’t follow.

Watermarking cannot certify that a passage is true, original or entirely written by one source.

Does it change answer quality?

The SynthID paper reports no statistically significant difference in user ratings across approximately 20 million Gemini responses in a live comparison. That supports the tested configuration, not a guarantee for every sentence. [3]

What about short answers, facts and code?

Short text offers few observations. Constrained outputs offer few choices. Both weaken detection. Code is not categorically exempt: comments and flexible parts can still carry a mark. [1]

Can copying, editing or translation remove it?

Copying the exact characters preserves a pattern carried by word choices. Edits can change both tokens and the context used to score nearby tokens. Paraphrasing, shortening and mixing sources can weaken the mark. A translation produced by a watermarking model may carry its own mark; survival of the original mark through another translator is a separate question. [1]

Can a watermark identify me or my chat?

Anthropic says its mark does not encode a person, organisation or conversation. That describes its scheme, not every possible watermark. Watermarks are also separate from a service’s logs and data policies. [1]

Can I check any text with this page?

No. These are educational simulations. A provider’s mark requires a matching detector. In its September 1, 2026 update, Anthropic described a detection API in private preview for eligible organisations. Access and model coverage may change. [1]

Are local models unmarked?

That depends on the generation setup. A local model can use a watermarking sampler too. Location alone does not determine whether output is marked. [4]

The idea to take with you

The signature is not a hidden character.
It is a repeated preference.

Each choice looks ordinary. A key lets a detector search across those choices for a pattern. Understanding the pattern also means recognising when the evidence is too weak to conclude anything.
