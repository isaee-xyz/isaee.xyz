Which way will these numbers end up?

This machine repeatedly compares pairs. Its first whole round puts the largest number at the front. That seems like an odd start for putting the smallest first.

Your prediction:

Make a guess to unlock the machine.

The largest number travels with a boundary.

Here is the rule: visit each position from left to right. For each one, compare its current value with every position from left to right. If its value is smaller than the value it meets, exchange the two records.

After every round, the section up to the marked boundary is in order, and a largest number sits at its right edge. At the final boundary, that section is the whole list.

Why does that promise survive the next round?

Start with an ordered section ending in a maximum. The next value walks through this section. When it is smaller than the value it meets, the two exchange places: the smaller stays behind and the larger continues. This inserts the new value into the section and carries the maximum to the new boundary. Later comparisons cannot dislodge that maximum because nothing is larger.

The first round establishes the maximum at position one. Each later round extends the ordered section by one. A fixed number of rounds makes the procedure stop. That is the beginning, the preserved promise, and the finish of the argument.

This is Stanley Fung’s surprising all-pairs algorithm, described in 2021. Its discovery is a human story. The AI story comes later. Read the formal verification ↗

What do the counters include?

A comparison tests two keys. Moving a record into one main-array slot counts as one write; a swap counts as two. These counters exclude scratch-buffer writes, digit inspection and bookkeeping. Counting and radix sort can therefore show zero comparisons while still doing work. Playback duration is not execution time.

Where do n² and n log n come from?

n means the number of records. Comparing every position with every position makes n × n comparisons. Doubling n makes that expression four times larger. A halving strategy has log₂ n levels at powers of two; doing a linear pass per level produces the n log₂ n growth term.

These are illustrative growth functions in abstract work units, not measured runtimes or exact counts for merge sort. O describes an upper bound; Θ describes a tight growth rate. Constants, input structure and the operations counted still matter. Read the analysis ↗

04 / THE SECOND SURPRISE

If we know how to sort,
what did AI discover?

Knowing a correct procedure does not tell us whether we can do the same job with fewer costly instructions.

In 2023, AlphaDev searched for small sorting programs at the level of machine instructions. It learned to build candidates that were correct and efficient. Small routines entered LLVM’s C++ standard library. Nature paper ↗

The discovery happened before deployment. A program can use the resulting routine without asking an AI model to sort each list.

This was an improvement to tiny building blocks. It did not invent sorting or remove the general limit on comparison-based sorting.

    A hint, then a solution

    Try neighbouring positions in this order: 1 ↔ 2, then 2 ↔ 3, then 1 ↔ 2. The middle comparison places the largest value last; the final one repairs the first pair. This is a classic sorting network, not an AI-discovered routine.

    A DIFFERENT RULE MAKES A DIFFERENT PUZZLE

    You may only flip
    the top of the stack.

    Make the smallest pancake sit on top and the largest on the bottom. Tap a pancake to flip everything from the top through that pancake.

    Five pancakes. One legal kind of move.

    How to think about it

    Find the largest unfinished pancake. Bring it to the top with one flip, then flip the unfinished stack to put it at the bottom. Leave that finished bottom alone and repeat.
